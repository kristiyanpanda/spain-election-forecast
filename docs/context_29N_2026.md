**Correction on the housing route.** It is the *Diputación Permanente* of Congress, not a Diputación
Provincial. It has 69 members (68 + the Speaker): PP 27, PSOE 25, Sumar 6, Vox 6, and ERC, Junts,
Bildu and the Mixed Group 1 each. If everyone votes as on 2 October, the first decree passes 35-34
**without Junts**. The second is harder because PNV voted against it. The Permanente has 30 days, so the
vote lands inside the pre-campaign. That makes it a campaign event as well as a legislative one.

**Electoral calendar (LOREG, counted from publication on ~6 Oct; check against the BOE):**
coalitions registered with the Junta Electoral within 10 days (~16 Oct), candidate lists by day 20
(~26 Oct), campaign 13-27 Nov, reflection day 28 Nov.

**Party supply, this week:**
- **Frente Amplio**, the new brand of Movimiento Sumar, IU, Más Madrid and the Comuns, which largely
  replaces the Sumar umbrella. The lead candidate and roadmap will be announced on **17 Oct**. In the

  minister in the CIS, though he keeps saying no), Mónica García (would have to leave the Madrid race),
  Ada Colau and Ernest Urtasun.
- **Podemos**: Irene Montero is the candidate. Podemos offers joint **open primaries on 14-15 Oct**,
  just before the coalition deadline. Without them it runs alone.
- **Rufián**: in July 2025 he proposed a plurinational front (ERC + Bildu + BNG + Ara Més, modelled on
  *Ahora Repúblicas*, which won about 5 % in the 2024 European election and beat Sumar). In May 2026 he
  said he was open to leading a broader front. ERC's leadership calls it "a personal initiative", and
  Sumar's coordinator said picking candidates "is not up to the ERC spokesperson". **No source I found
  confirms Rufián joining Frente Amplio.** Keep it as a scenario, not a base case.
- **Catalonia**: Aliança Catalana overtook Junts in the CEO regional poll (July) but **will not run on
  29N**. Junts is projected at 4-5 seats. AC's would-be voters split about 23 % Junts and 22 % Vox.
- **SALF** (Alvise) is at about 1.9 % and fading.

Patterns that matter for the analysis:
1. **The bloc gap is about 15 pp**, against 1.5 pp in 2023. Movement is within the right (PP and Vox as
   communicating vessels). The left is not losing voters to the right so much as **to abstention**.
   Raw direct vote still puts PSOE ahead of PP (about 23-25 vs 19-21). The gap appears once pollsters
   apply turnout weighting, which makes **mobilisation the main lever** (infopolitica). One
   (unverified, partisan) report puts PSOE losses to abstention at about 600k in September alone.
2. **Agenda conflict.** Private pollsters show immigration as the top problem since Ceuta, while the CIS
   shows housing. The election is a fight over which issue is salient: Sánchez has framed it as a vote
   on housing, while PP and Vox will frame it around corruption and immigration.
3. **Ceuta blame.** 89 % blame Morocco and 72 % the Spanish government, but the split is partisan
   (PSOE voters 48.5 % vs PP 90.8 %). Sánchez has a -48.5 net rating on the crisis and Feijóo -31.0, so
   the crisis hurt both main parties and helped Vox. Vox is seen as most competent on immigration
   (27 %, PSOE 18.4 %, PP 13.9 %). Seventy per cent want tighter border control, including 52 % of PSOE
   voters.

## 2.2 Peripheral parties: how their seats are decided

**Mechanics.** There is no national threshold. Each of the 52 provinces runs its own D'Hondt count,
with a 3 % threshold *in that province*. A party with 1-2 % nationally but 15-30 % in its own
provinces converts votes into seats far more efficiently than Sumar or Podemos at 6 %. The seats
at stake:

| region | provinces (seats) | main peripheral contest |
|---|---|---|
| Catalonia (48) | Barcelona 32, Tarragona 6, Girona 6, Lleida 4 | ERC vs Junts vs PSC; Aliança Catalana **absent** on 29N |
| Basque Country (18) | Bizkaia 8, Gipuzkoa 6, Araba 4 | PNV vs EH Bildu |
| Navarre (5) | Navarra 5 | Bildu, UPN (inside PP?), PSN |
| Galicia (23) | A Coruña 8, Pontevedra 7, Lugo 4, Ourense 4 | BNG's second seat |
| Canarias (15) | Las Palmas 8, S.C. Tenerife 7 | CC's single seat |

2023 baseline: ERC 7, Junts 7, Bildu 6, PNV 5, BNG 1, CC 1, UPN 1.

**What the latest regional evidence says:**
- **Catalonia, CEO (May-June 2026), Congress question:** PSC 17-18, **ERC 10-11** (up from 7), Vox 6-7,
  PP 5-6, **Junts 4-6** (down from 7). In the Catalan Parliament question AC (23-25) overtakes Junts
  (16-18). With AC not running on 29N, about 23 % of its voters go to Junts and 22 % to Vox. That
  softens Junts's fall but does not reverse it. *Expect: Junts loses momentum and seats; ERC gains.*
  The no on housing is a gamble on owner-heavy Catalan conservative voters. Its effect is unmeasured
  because the CEO fieldwork predates it.
- **Basque Country, Sociómetro (Apr 2026, regional question):** PNV 35.3 % vs Bildu 32.2 %, a
  near-tie. At the 2023 general election Bildu matched PNV in seats (6 vs 5). *Expect: a fight for
  the marginal seat in Bizkaia and Araba. Bildu also gains from PSOE-to-left movement in Gipuzkoa and
  Navarre.* PNV's vote against the second housing decree makes it the swing vote in the Permanente.
- **Galicia:** BNG has been strong regionally since 2024. A second seat in A Coruña is the upside.
- **The CIS national sample is useless here.** Junts at 0.5 % direct vote means about 20 respondents.
  National polls set the level for these parties very noisily.

**What our model does now (and why it will under-read ERC):** national poll share × 2023 provincial
share (proportional swing) + log-normal provincial noise, then provincial D'Hondt
(`src/electoral/seats.py`). Peripheral parties get *only* national-poll information, so they inherit
national sampling noise and miss region-specific shifts. Our 18 Sep nowcast gave ERC 6-9 and
Junts 3-6, against the CEO's 10-11 and 4-6. → **Re-run proposal:** add a regional-poll layer (CEO for
Catalonia, Sociómetro / Deustobarómetro for Euskadi, CIS regional subsamples from microdata) that
updates peripheral shares at the regional level, and treat AC's absence explicitly.

## 3. Theories that apply

Each item gives the mechanism, then what it predicts for 29N, then what it means for the model.

### 3.1 Election timing
- **Endogenous timing** (Smith 2004, *Election Timing*; Kayser 2005; Schleiter & Tavits 2016, *JOP*).
  Leaders who *choose* a good moment win a bonus, and an early call can signal expected decline.
  29N is not an opportunistic call. It is a **forced or survival dissolution** after losing the
  legislative majority. Sánchez's July 2023 call is the template: he called it right after a regional
  defeat, turned it into a referendum on PP + Vox, and beat the polls.
  → *Model*: there is no timing bonus to add. Horizon risk shrinks from 11 months to 8 weeks.
- **Campaign effects / "enlightened preferences"** (Gelman & King 1993). Campaigns move voters towards
  fundamentals. Here the fundamentals point two ways: strong growth on one side, cost of ruling,
  scandals and regional losses on the other.

### 3.2 Issue ownership and salience
- **Issue ownership** (Petrocik 1996) and **salience theory** (Budge & Farlie 1983). Parties win by
  making the issues they own salient. The left owns housing and the right owns immigration and
  corruption. The Diputación Permanente vote is Sánchez's tool for keeping housing on the agenda.
- **Focusing events** (Birkland 1997) and **securitisation**. Ceuta is a textbook focusing event. Its
  salience decays (it is already falling in the CIS), and 93 % of those who entered have left. The
  question is whether it persists until 29N.
- **Housing as a new cleavage** (Ansell 2014, *APSR*; Ansell 2019, *ARPS*). Owners lean more
  conservative and renters are a young, mobile and abstention-prone group. Spain's tenure divide is
  generational: under-35 renters versus owners over 55. Housing works for the left only if it
  **mobilises renters**, not if it persuades owners.

### 3.3 Corruption voting
- **Limited punishment.** Anduiza, Gallego & Muñoz (2013, *CPS*) find partisans tolerate their own
  side's corruption. Fernández-Vázquez, Barberá & Rivero (2016, *PSRM*) find Spanish voters punish
  corruption that brings no local benefit and forgive it otherwise. De Vries & Solaz (2017, *ARPS*)
  review the evidence: punishment averages only 1-4 pp, and it depends on clarity and on whether a
  credible alternative exists.
- **Clarity of responsibility** (Powell & Whitten 1993). A minority government that depends on seven
  partners blurs responsibility, which lets Sánchez blame Junts and the "blockade" for failures. It
  also lets the right blame him for the decrees that fell.
  → *Prediction*: scandals act mainly through **demobilisation of PSOE voters**, not through switching
  to PP. That matches the abstention pattern above.

### 3.4 Polarisation and bloc voting
- **Affective polarisation**: Spain ranks near the top of Europe (Gidron, Adams & Horne 2020; Torcal &
  Comellas 2022; Miller 2020). Negative partisanship keeps voters inside their bloc.
- **Two-bloc competition** (Orriols & León 2020; Rodríguez-Teruel 2020). Volatility is high *within*
  blocs and low *between* them, which our descriptive Pedersen split confirms.
- **Counter-mobilisation against the radical right.** April 2019 (Vox's entry, 75.8 % turnout) and
  July 2023 show the left mobilising when a PP + Vox government becomes concrete. In 2026 that
  government is no longer hypothetical: PP + Vox governments exist in Extremadura and Aragón, and the
  fear argument may be weaker through **normalisation** (Mudde 2019; Akkerman, de Lange & Rooduijn
  2016) or stronger through visible policy.

### 3.5 Electoral system and coordination (key for Frente Amplio)
- **Mechanical and psychological effects** (Duverger; Cox 1997, *Making Votes Count*). The median
  Spanish district elects about 5 seats, so the effective threshold is roughly 10-15 % in most
  provinces. A party at 6 % nationally only wins seats in Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia and a few
  others. Splitting the left of PSOE costs seats roughly in proportion to how many provinces sit
  between the two lists' thresholds.
- **"Two plus two isn't four."** Electoral alliances lose voters. Podemos + IU took 6.1 M votes in 2015
  as separate lists and Unidos Podemos 5.05 M together in 2016, about -17 % (Rama Caamaño & Cordero
  2018; Orriols & Cordero 2016). Sumar 2023 (12.3 %, Podemos included) against Unidas Podemos +
  Más País 2019 (about 15.2 %) shows the same.
- **Brand dilution** (Lupu 2016, *Party Brands in Crisis*). Repeated mergers, renamings and splits
  wear down partisan attachment. Frente Amplio is the fourth brand in this space in ten years.
- **New-party success** (Sikk 2012, "newness" as a winning formula; Tavits 2008; Hino 2012). Newness
  helps when the party offers *a new face plus a new issue*. A rebrand of the same partners helps less,
  unless the candidate is new (Bustinduy) and the issue is fresh (housing).
- **Rufián's plurinational front** cuts across the centre-periphery cleavage (Lipset & Rokkan 1967).
  ERC, Bildu and BNG voters are partly *nationalist* rather than *left*, and a state-wide list could
  lose them to abstention or Junts. That is probably why ERC's leadership refuses.

### 3.6 Peripheral parties and outbidding
- **Ethnic and nationalist outbidding** (Rabushka & Shepsle 1972; Zuber & Szöcsiová 2015). Junts's
  break with the government (Oct 2025) and its no on housing are best read as outbidding against
  Aliança Catalana. AC's absence on 29N helps Junts, and Catalan provinces are where the
  investiture is decided.
- **Punishing whoever topples the government**: see Portugal 2022 in §4. The coalition that brings a
  government down is often blamed. Whether Junts's voters, who skew towards owners, blame it is open.

### 3.7 Cost of ruling and economic voting
- Incumbents lose about 1.5-2.5 pp per term on average (Paldam 1986; Stevenson 2002), with
  thermostatic public opinion (Wlezien 1995) pulling the same way. Macro growth is strong, but
  **economic voting** (Lewis-Beck & Stegmaier 2000) is sociotropic *and* pocketbook. Housing costs
  are the pocketbook channel that cancels out the macro good news. Regional "second-order" elections
  (Reif & Schmitt 1980) already punished PSOE, and they are a noisy but informative signal.

### 3.8 Polling error and turnout models
- 2023 is the cautionary case: every pollster underestimated PSOE by about 5 pp (our backtest). The
  mechanism matches today's raw-vs-estimated gap: likely-voter models built on past turnout miss
  late mobilisation. Gap 1996 is the historical twin (§4).

## 4. Comparable cases

| case | trigger | what happened | lesson for 29N |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spain 1996** | CiU withdrew support, budget failed, PSOE corruption (GAL, Filesa, Roldán) | Polls had PP far ahead; result PP 38.8 vs PSOE 37.6, the *dulce derrota* | Closest twin: Catalan partner defects, scandals, and a mobilised left narrows a big poll gap |
| **Spain Apr 2019** | ERC and PDeCAT voted down the budget | PSOE won (28.7 %), turnout 75.8 %, Vox's entry mobilised the left | Defeat by peripheral partners can be turned into a mandate campaign |
| **Spain Jul 2023** | Snap call after the May regional rout | Polls missed PSOE by about 5 pp; PP + Vox fell short | Mobilisation against a PP + Vox government; polling bias towards the right |
| **Portugal 2022** | BE and PCP voted down Costa's budget | PS absolute majority (41.4 %); BE and PCP roughly halved | Voters punished the *topplers* and rewarded the incumbent asking for stability |
| **Portugal 2024** | Costa resigned over the Influencer corruption probe | AD 28.8 vs PS 28.0; Chega 18 % | Corruption plus immigration salience feeds the radical right more than the main opposition |
| **Portugal 2025** | Montenegro lost a confidence vote (Spinumviva scandal) | Incumbent AD *grew*; PS collapsed to 22.8 %; Chega came second in seats | The party that forced the election was punished; the scandal-hit incumbent survived |
| **Germany 2025** | Traffic-light coalition collapsed over the budget | SPD 16.4 % (worst ever); AfD 20.8 %; Die Linke 8.8 % on a rent/housing campaign and young voters; BSW 4.97 %, just under the threshold | A **housing-focused, new-face radical left** can surge in weeks; splinters at the threshold waste votes |
| **France 2024** | Macron dissolved after the EU election | Nouveau Front Populaire formed in **4 days** with one programme and came first in seats | Fast left unity under threat is feasible; the 2-round system made it pay more than D'Hondt would |
| **Italy 2022** | Draghi fell | The centre-left split three ways; the right took about 59 % of seats on about 44 % of votes | The cost of a coordination failure under district-based rules |
| **Netherlands 2023/2025** | 2023: cabinet fell over asylum. 2025: PVV left the cabinet over asylum | 2023: PVV won, GL-PvdA joint list 25 seats. 2025: PVV lost ground, GL-PvdA fell to 20, D66 won | Mergers don't add up; immigration salience is volatile; the party that brings the government down can be punished |
| **Austria 2019** | Ibiza video | FPÖ -9.8, ÖVP +6 | Scandals hit hardest when responsibility is clear and personal |
| **UK 2017** | Opportunistic snap call by May | A 20-point lead shrank; a young-renter surge for Labour | Campaigns can move a lot in 7 weeks; renters can be mobilised |

The comparative record suggests two competing narratives for 29N:
**(A) "Portugal 2022 / Spain 2019"**: the incumbent blames the toppling partner and asks for a mandate.
**(B) "Portugal 2024 / Germany 2025"**: scandals, fatigue and immigration salience pull the incumbent
down and boost the radical right. Current polls look like (B). The left's hope is the 1996/2023 pattern,
mobilisation that the polls miss.

## 5. Implications for the forecast re-run (to discuss before coding)

1. **Election date is fixed**: horizon 29 Nov 2026 (about 8 weeks), so the 11-month drift term
   collapses. The election-day error term (12 % of share) becomes the dominant source of uncertainty.
2. **Party mapping**: add `frente_amplio` in the Sumar lineage (the break is similar to the Podemos
   split). Podemos either stays separate or merges after the 14-15 Oct primaries. **Run both
   scenarios**, and apply a merger-efficiency prior when merging (2016: about 0.83 of the sum).
3. **Rufián scenario**: low probability. If it happens, ERC's vote moves partly into a state-wide list,
   which needs a province-level allocation, since ERC's vote is about 100 % Catalan.
4. **Junts**: AC is absent, so Catalan polls with and without AC need adjusting.
5. **Correlated left-bloc error**: given the 2023 miss and the raw-vs-estimated gap, test whether an
   asymmetric or bloc-correlated polling error is justified by the backtest. Don't assume it.
6. **CIS (decided 5 Oct)**: main source is the **CIS microdata from cis.es, re-weighted by us** (2023
   recall + likely voters, §2.1). The official CIS estimate is not the anchor; it may enter as a
   separate series with its own house effect. Fix the parser bug: "CIS (re-estimator)" rows on
   Wikipedia were being merged into "CIS". Regional institutional polls (CEO, Sociómetro) are also
   main sources; private polls are complementary. Improving the weighting is deferred to a later study.
7. **Events to timestamp** in the trend chart: Ceuta (30 Jul), decrees rejected (2 Oct), dissolution
   (5 Oct), Diputación Permanente vote (~mid Oct), Frente Amplio candidate (17 Oct).
8. **Peripheral parties**: add a regional-poll layer (CEO, Sociómetro) as described in §2.2.
9. **Re-run date: 17 Oct 2026**, after the coalition deadline (~16 Oct) and the Frente Amplio announcement.
